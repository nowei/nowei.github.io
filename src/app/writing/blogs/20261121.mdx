<Text>
    There is a term coined about how Donald Trump talks called "the Weave", where he takes
    the listener on a journey through his mind. He talks about whatever he wants to talk about
    and by the end of it, all the disparate parts are supposed to come together into some
    overarching message. There are many things about this man that I disagree with, but I believe
    that this is actually the method of communication that I also find myself taking a lot of the time.
    My mind tends to hop around to different topics and ideas or go off in tangents. This is great
    when things are uninteresting, but not so much when I'm supposed to pay attention to things
    going on around me. I also find that I end up repeating myself quite often,
    which I think is something that I need to consciously work on, since it adds
    nothing to the conversation.
</Text>
<Text>
    Accordingly, I warn the reader that I may abruptly switch topics every few paragraphs.
</Text>
<Text>
    One thing I like to talk to people about, especially people who are a few years
    removed from college, is the idea that back in our college days (it is interesting
    that this is almost universal) many of us were bright-eyed and bushy-tailed and had our
    sights set on changing the world. Or perhaps making the world a better place. Then we graduated
    and reality sets in. There's rent (or a mortgage), bills, there's family obligations, or maybe
    you're just struggling to figure out what to eat or do today, and "there's a million things
    I haven't done(!)".
</Text>
<Text>
    The image of what we would do in the future from when we were in college probably
    does not line up with what folks are doing today. I believe that this gap in perception
    between your expectations and reality is what causes many people to feel discontent
    in their day-to-day lives. Should we tell college-aged folks to temper their expectations?
    Or should we figure out how to move towards the dreams and aspirations we had just a few years
    prior? The former is likely more realistic, but the latter is idealistic in a way that sparks joy.
    I think the real answer (to most things, really) is like the cop-out answer of "it depends"
    or some "middle-of-the-road" solution like, "you must balance your expectations with your ideals
    in a way that does not invite internal conflict".
</Text>
<Text>
    In the real world, there are a lot of tricks that people do to come off as
    academic or intelligent or like they have some sage wisdom or some grand understanding of
    the universe when they aren't actually saying anything. Given this, I think I'd
    recommend staying away from anyone trying to sell you a course or whatever
    on the internet, especially if they say that it'll fundamentally change how you live your life.
    There are probably things that people should hear, but if you end up getting much more out
    of it from some internet person saying it vs. going to like a doctor/therapist or talking to your
    friends and family about it, there might be some other problems you want to address first.
</Text>
<Text>
    I would hope that folks strive for the latter though, but it may be too much to ask for. I think
    that part of the issue is that we are inconvenienced in our lives in such a way that we
    cannot go out into the world and be free to venture forth into the great unknown. I think
    in a prosperous society, people should be able to make bets without losing the proverbial farm.
    There are a lot of well-off people who are able to make bets and try their far-fetched ideas
    because for them, there is a safety net. They have something to fall back on in case their
    ventures fail. Sometimes it takes a few attempts before they can find something that sticks for
    them. Like it is great to be able to say these great and successful people are amazing because
    they made a bet and it panned out for them. But the luxury of making bets is not afforded
    to everyone. For many, the stability of their lives is put into jeopardy because they decided
    to go get Taco Bell at 3AM on a random Wednesday or if their kid asks them to buy a pizza
    from PizzaHut to celebrate something.
</Text>

<Text>
    Outside of having the financial/psychological security of the well-to-do, the inconveniences
    I generally refer to include things and situations from our daily lives.
    By inconveniences, I mean that every day, we suffer a million cuts. The tiny inconveniences
    add up over time, like the bus that is 5 minutes late or early, causing you to miss something;
    the healthcare system requiring you to jump through hoops with the insurance company to figure
    out whether some operation or medication will be a covered expense or if it'll be a;
    or making some mundane decision like whether you should get a brownie or a cookie becuase you
    can only afford one (maybe from a health goals standpoint or maybe monetarily).
</Text>
<Text>
    There is a dichotomy in terms of issues that are personal and those that are systemic.
    For inconveniences that are personal, they can usually be addressed with routines.
    Although some systemic issues make it harder to build a routine, which is also understandable.
    The systemic issues usually need to be addressed on a higher level, like healthcare reform,
    aging electrical grids, and equitable systems. We may expand on this in a future blog post,
    so I will leave it here for me to pick back up on a future, indeterminate date.
</Text>

<Text>
    One phase that has stuck to me recently is: "May you live in interesting times".
    I do not know anyone who wishes to live in interesting times. In fact, please give me as
    uninteresting a time as you possibly can.
</Text>
<Text>
    I think we, as people, crave both novelty and stability. In times of turmoil, we want things
    to be consistent and stable. In times of tranquility, we want things to be interesting and fluid.
    I think this kind of speaks to the nature of people, like we think that the grass is always greener
    on the other side. In some instances, maybe we've never been able to cross the fence if this is
    all we've known. I think it's easier to want to want change when your life is already fairly stable
    and we want things to be stable when we feel like the chaos is coming a bit too much. I guess in
    life we want a mixture of both to keep things interesting, but as much as we would like
    it to be true, we likely don't have as much control over the things in our lives as we would like.
    From the jobs we work to the people we meet, it's all kind of up to the RNG of the universe to
    determine the outcome. Surely we can take some steps to influence the outcome, but at the end
    of the day, things just happen.
</Text>

<Text>
    The title of the post is a burning star because that's what it feels like to live
    at the moment.

<Text>
    I have to consistently remind myself that life is a marathon, not a race.
    We all have to go at our own pace, but I'm sure that each and every one of us
    knows one or two people who desire to rush through things or to move things along.
    We may even be guilty of doing it ourselves at various times.
</Text>
<Text>
    But maybe the goal shouldn't be to try to shine bright.
    Maybe it's to shine as dimly as possible so that you can survive the journey to
    your eventual, final destination? Or it's the other way around and it's that the journey
    is the destination.
</Text>
<Text>
    It is unclear whether I feel like I am burnt out or if the general vibe of the times is
    just that people are checked out and offloading as much stuff as they can, either to
    people or otherwise. Since the more baggage we bring, the harder it is
    to make the journey, whatever it may be. Interestingly, on the other side of the coin,
    we live in a time when things have never historically been easier to do or learn, we
    just have to find the self-efficacy to feel like we're able to do it, the time to practice,
    and then finally stage to perform.
</Text>
<Text>
    There may be no one to witness it, but if you want an audience, there are those who are willing
    to lend their eyes and years to bear witness.
</Text>
